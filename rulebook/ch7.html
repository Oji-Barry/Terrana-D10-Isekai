Introduction

Every character in this game is a person first and a set of numbers second. Before you touch your dice pools or dot allocations, you need to know who you are playing. Tropes and Driving Factors are the two pieces of your character that answer that question most directly.

Your Trope is your personality in shorthand. Your Virtues and Vices are your values, set on seven scales. Your Driving Factors are your reasons for getting out of bed, and they come from the strongest of those values. Together they are the lens through which every mechanical choice you make should be viewed. The fighter who chose Battle Maniac and High Wrath is a fundamentally different person from the fighter who chose Reluctant Hero and High Patience, even if their dots end up looking similar on paper. None of this constrains your character; it gives them shape.

You pick one Trope in Step 1 of character creation (Part 1 of this chapter). In Step 2 you set your seven scales and choose two Driving Factors, one for each of your highs (Part 2).

Part 1: Tropes

A Trope is a recognizable personality archetype, the kind that anyone who has watched enough anime or read enough light novels will identify immediately. They are not stereotypes; they are starting points. You do not have to choose a Trope from this list. If your character's personality does not map cleanly to any of these, work with your Storyteller to create something that fits. The list is a resource, not a rulebook.

Each Trope provides a Boon and a Bane. Note them in the specialty field next to the relevant abilities on your sheet.

The Boon: One bonus die on rolls with the chosen ability within the Trope's category. It stacks with any specialty already on that ability.

The Bane: One penalty die on rolls with the fixed ability the Trope names.

Virtues and Vices: Your scale positions also add or remove dice, depending on whether you are acting with or against them: 1 die at a low, 2 dice at a high. These stack with your Boon or Bane when both apply. This is why the scales on your sheet are marked 2, 1, 0, 1, 2.

The Tropes are grouped by their Boon: each ability group has its own table, with two Tropes for every ability, in the same order as on your character sheet. A few more Tropes follow in Extra Tropes.

Part 2: Virtues, Vices, and the Seven Scales

People are not consistent. They have values they hold deeply and impulses they struggle to control. They have principles they uphold under pressure and principles they abandon when the cost gets high enough. The Seven Scales put all of that on the table and make it mechanical.

The scales do not tell you who your character is. They reflect who your character is right now, and they change as your character changes. A deeply compassionate person can, through choices made across sessions and arcs, shift toward something darker. A dangerously self-serving one can find their way toward something better. The scales move because people move.

The Seven Scales

Each scale runs between a Virtue and a Vice. Neither end is simply good or bad. They are two orientations toward the same human experience, and either can produce heroism or destruction depending on who carries it and why.

Each scale has five positions, marked 2, 1, 0, 1, 2 on your sheet. The number is how many dice the position adds to rolls that fit it, and removes from rolls that go against it.

Scale Positions

PositionMarkEffect
High Virtue2+2 dice on rolls that align with this Virtue. -2 dice when you try to act against it.
Low Virtue1+1 die on rolls that align with this Virtue. -1 die when you try to act against it.
Central0No bonus and no drawback. You are balanced on this scale.
Low Vice1+1 die on rolls that align with this Vice. -1 die when you try to act against it.
High Vice2+2 dice on rolls that align with this Vice. -2 dice when you try to act against it.

These dice only apply when the scale genuinely matters to what your character is doing. A character at High Humility does not get bonus dice on every social roll, only when acting with real humility: deferring to others, admitting limits, putting someone else's needs ahead of their own recognition. The Storyteller decides when a scale is relevant. Scale dice stack with your Trope's Boon or Bane when both apply.

Starting Positions

At character creation (Step 2), set your seven scales:

Two highs: two scales at High Virtue or High Vice.

Two lows: two scales at Low Virtue or Low Vice.

Three central: the other three scales stay at Central.

Then choose two Driving Factors, one for each of your highs, matching the scale and the side the high sits on. Every drive is listed with its scale in the Full Reference at the end of this chapter. The names are starting points: what matters is what your drive is at its core, and which scale and side it belongs to. If you have a custom drive in mind, work with your Storyteller to place it.

Your highs also set your starting Willpower: both Vices start you at 4, both Virtues at 6, and one of each at 5. This only applies at creation. Once play begins, moving on the scales never changes your Willpower rating.

Central scales are not inert. They are simply waiting.

Consulting the Scales

Before committing to a significant action, players are encouraged to consult the scales: look at where your character sits on each relevant scale and let that shape how they weigh the choice.

Consulting the scales is a roleplay tool first and a mechanical tool second. A character at High Patience thinking about charging into a fight does not need a roll to know that charging runs against who they are right now. They can feel it. Consulting the scales makes that feeling explicit at the table.

The Storyteller may ask how your character is weighing the decision. What does their position tell them to do? What does the situation seem to demand? Are those the same thing? The answer is where interesting roleplay lives.

A character never has to act in line with their scales. People struggle, slip, and sometimes act against their own values. But they should know when they are doing it, and so should the player.

When Rolls Are Triggered

A Virtue or Vice roll can come up in any situation where a character's position on a scale genuinely matters: combat, social encounters, exploration, or quiet moments between scenes. The Storyteller decides when a roll is called for. The most common triggers are:

Temptation: a test or temptation that directly engages the character's position on a scale.

Acting against: the character tries to act against their current position on a relevant scale.

Acting with: the character acts in strong alignment with their position. The bonus dice simply apply, with no extra roll.

A deliberate choice: the Storyteller presents a decision meant to engage one or more scales, and the character's choice decides whether their position helps or hinders them.

Actions are the main trigger. Words count as actions, physical deeds count, and deliberately doing nothing when action was possible counts too, especially on scales like Diligence and Patience where inaction is itself a choice.

When a roll is called for, the Storyteller names the scale and whether you are rolling with or against your position, and you add or remove dice accordingly. Success usually means the character can act without betraying their principles. Failure usually means Willpower or Sanity damage, depending on how serious the violation is.

Acting Against Your Scales

When a character acts against their position on a scale, the cost depends on how serious the violation is and how strongly they hold that position.

Consequences

PositionMinor ViolationSevere Violation
High Virtue or High Vice1 Willpower damage1 or more Sanity damage, at the Storyteller's discretion
Low Virtue or Low Vice1 Willpower damage1 Willpower damage. Never causes Sanity damage.
CentralNo consequenceNo consequence

Lows can only ever cost Willpower, no matter how serious the violation. The character feels the tension, but it does not break them. Highs cost more, because the character's identity is built around them. Acting against a high is not just a bad decision; it is a betrayal of something the character has built their sense of self around.

A character at High Humility who publicly boasts about their achievements is acting against the deepest part of who they are right now. A character at High Pride forced to grovel, genuinely submitting rather than putting on a show, suffers the same way. Taking quiet pride in being humble is fine. It is the act of boasting that triggers the cost, not the feeling.

How the Scales Shift

The scales move in response to the choices made in play. The Storyteller tracks shifts and announces them.

When a choice warrants a shift, the Storyteller tells the player right away that their scales have shifted. How far the dot moves is revealed at the end of the story chapter, giving the player time to reflect on what led to it before the sheet changes.

Most shifts move a dot one position. A dramatic choice can move it as far as the Storyteller judges fitting, even across the center or from one end of the scale to the other. How far it moves depends on the action and how big a violation of the character's position it was.

The size of the action sets the size of the shift. A character at High Lust who turns down someone they have no interest in triggers nothing. The same character who genuinely wants someone and refuses, again and again, to act on it, denying their nature rather than being patient, may find their dot drifting toward Central or beyond.

Every shift, in either direction, does damage. Small shifts cause Willpower damage; significant shifts cause Sanity damage. A character's sense of self is shaken when their values move, even toward something better. Change is costly. Shifts never change the character's Willpower rating itself.

Losing and Gaining Drives

Your Driving Factors are tied to your highs. If a high shifts away from its end of the scale, you lose that Drive. It is no longer what gets you out of bed in the morning.

If any other scale shifts all the way to a high, you gain a new Drive. The Storyteller assigns it based on the choices that caused the shift and which end you moved toward. It is not the player's choice in the moment; the Storyteller responds to what the character actually did across the arc. There is no limit: a character can have as many Drives as they have highs.

A character who begins play driven by Compassion and ends a long campaign driven by something much darker has not failed. They have changed, and the system makes that change visible.

If a player disagrees with how their scales shifted, or with the Drive assigned to a new high, they should say so. The Storyteller has the final say but should listen to the player's view of their own character. If the disagreement holds, the table reviews the specific choices together. The scales work best when everyone agrees on what they mean.

The Scales in Play

High Patience in Combat

Rael sits at High Patience, driven by Steadfast. His party faces mercenaries who have cornered a group of civilians, and one of them taunts Rael, calling him a coward for holding back. Charging in to silence the taunt would go against his position, and the Storyteller reminds the player that Rael would take 2 penalty dice to do it. The player has Rael hold his ground, absorb the taunt, and wait for the right moment. Because he is acting in line with his scale, no roll is needed, and his 2 bonus dice apply to his next coordinated action with the party.

Central, No Roll

Mirren is Central on Kindness and Envy. A rival has just received an honor she wanted. The Storyteller calls for no roll: she has no strong orientation to act with or against, so she can feel and respond however she likes without mechanical consequence.

A Shift in Progress

Davan is at High Charity, driven by Generosity. Across an arc he made several choices that put his own gain ahead of others, justifying each in the moment. At the end of the arc the Storyteller tells him his scales have shifted: his Charity and Greed dot has moved from High Charity to Central. He loses Generosity and takes Sanity damage. The player and Storyteller agree the move reflects what happened in the story.

Gaining a New Drive

Saya began play Central on Patience and Wrath. Over two arcs she kept choosing confrontation and meeting injustice with force. At the end of the second arc the Storyteller moves her to High Wrath and assigns her a new Drive, Righteous, because her anger was always aimed at things that deserved it. She takes Sanity damage, and she now has three Drives.

Disagreement at the Table

The Storyteller shifts a character two positions toward High Pride after a session where they publicly celebrated a victory. The player disagrees: the celebration felt earned. The table walks through the moments the Storyteller flagged and agrees the celebration was fine, but that brushing off a teammate's contribution was the real trigger. The shift is reduced to one position, and the game goes on.

ResentmentEnvyYou want what others have and cannot stand that they have it. Your envy poisons your relationships and makes the world feel smaller. Purely self-serving and destructive.

Diligence / Sloth

This scale governs the character's relationship with effort, engagement, and rest. Diligence is the orientation of work and purpose. Sloth is the orientation of stillness and disengagement.
